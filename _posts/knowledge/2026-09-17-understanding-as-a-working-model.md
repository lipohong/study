## Core idea

Understanding is stronger than recalling a statement or producing a fluent paraphrase. A useful working standard is whether a learner can use an explicit or implicit model to:

1. **Explain** the relevant relationship or mechanism.
2. **Predict** what should happen when stated conditions change.
3. **Apply or transfer** the relation to a new case.
4. **Inspect and revise assumptions** when a result conflicts with the model.

Learning therefore requires more than receiving a correction: state the old relation, locate the earliest failed assumption or missing condition, revise the smallest affected part, integrate it with what still holds, and test the reconstructed model in another case.

For stronger evidence, also ask whether the learner can preserve the core relation when translating between representations, identify a boundary or counterexample, and, in communication tasks, distinguish literal wording from context-supported inferences about intent.

## Practical distinction

| Performance | What it may show | Why it is insufficient by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Recites a formula or rule | Recall | Does not show the rule can guide a new judgment. |
| Gives a fluent explanation | Possible conceptual language | May merely paraphrase the rule without conditions or mechanism. |
| Predicts correctly once | Possible pattern recognition or a correct model | Could be a guess or a memorised example. |
| Explains, predicts under changed conditions, applies, and revises | Stronger evidence of understanding | Tests whether a model is usable rather than merely verbal. |

## Conditional-claim check

When evaluating a causal statement, ask:

1. What outcome is being explained?
2. What variable is claimed to influence it?
3. Which other variables or conditions matter?
4. What is assumed fixed?
5. What different outcome would occur if an important condition changed?

## Example

Overstrong claim: “讀書時間越長，成績一定越好。”

A better claim must specify conditions such as learning method, attention, rest, prior knowledge, and alignment between practice and assessment. It should describe a tendency or a relationship under those conditions, rather than claim an unconditional certainty.
