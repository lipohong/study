## Recurring useful ideas

- Understanding is more than recall; it concerns relations, mechanisms, and meaningful connections.
- New material can be related to prior models through comparison and analogy.
- A concept can be expressed in more than one representation, such as prose, diagram, example, or causal structure.
- Counterexamples, conditions, and contextual assumptions help define a concept's boundary.
- Understanding tasks can include causal/system reasoning and communication/pragmatic interpretation.

## Corrections and limits

- “Knowledge is points and understanding is lines” is a useful teaching image, but not a definition. Knowledge itself can include relations and structures; understanding is demonstrated use and revision of a coherent relevant model.
- An “aha” feeling, fluent wording, or one successful analogy is not sufficient evidence of understanding.
- Analogy can support model construction, but the learner must state the shared relation and the point where the analogy fails.
- Visualisation can reduce load and expose structure, but a diagram does not itself establish understanding.
- Repeated “why” questions generate causal hypotheses; they do not by themselves prove a root cause.
- Perspective-taking is relevant to understanding communicative meaning and intent, but it is not a required dimension of every causal, mathematical, or procedural understanding task.
- Statements about neural mechanisms, dopamine, universal effects of cross-disciplinary study, or “the fastest” way to learn are not adopted as established facts without appropriate evidence.
